# Conclusion

Short. Five to eight sentences. Its only jobs are to leave the reader with the
transferable idea and to close cleanly.

## Structure

```
1. What problem, and the core idea that solved it — in your own compressed words,
   not copy-pasted from the abstract.
2. The strongest single piece of evidence.
3. The transferable knowledge: what a reader now knows or can do differently.
4. The scope boundary, in one sentence (if Limitations is separate, one pointer).
5. One concrete next step.
```

## Rules

- **No new claims.** Anything asserted here must already be established. A
  conclusion is the worst place to introduce a claim, because reviewers read it
  last and remember it.
- **No new citations.**
- **Do not restate the abstract.** Different words, different emphasis. The
  abstract sells; the conclusion consolidates.
- **No hedged grandiosity.** "We believe this work opens promising avenues toward
  general intelligence" damages an otherwise good paper.
- Sentence 3 is the one that matters. If you cannot write it — if the only
  takeaway is "our numbers are higher" — that is information about the paper, and
  it is worth surfacing at stage 9 rather than papering over.

## Template

```
This paper addressed <problem> by <core idea>. The key insight is <insight>,
which enables <property>. Across <datasets>, this yields <headline result>, and
an ablation confirms the gain comes from <mechanism> rather than <confound>.
The approach is bounded to <scope>; extending it to <setting> requires
<what specifically>, which we see as the natural next step.
```

## Checks

1. Any claim here not already supported earlier?
2. Any new citation?
3. Is it under half a column?
4. Does sentence 3 state something transferable, beyond "we scored higher"?
5. Is the scope boundary present and specific?
